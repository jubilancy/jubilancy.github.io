# Heuristic

Heuristic

Problem-solving method

For other uses, see [Heuristic (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic_(disambiguation) "Heuristic (disambiguation)").

A **heuristic**[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-1) or **heuristic technique** ( _[problem solving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_solving "Problem solving")_, _[mental shortcut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic_(psychology) "Heuristic (psychology)")_, _[rule of thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_thumb "Rule of thumb")_)[2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-2)[3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-3)[4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-4)[5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-5) is any approach to problem solving that employs a [pragmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatism "Pragmatism") method that is not necessarily [optimized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_optimisation "Mathematical optimisation"), perfected, or [rationalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationality "Rationality"), but is nevertheless "good enough" as an [approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximation "Approximation") or [attribute substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attribute_substitution "Attribute substitution").[6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-6)[7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-7) Where finding an optimal solution is impossible or impractical, heuristic methods can be used to speed up the process of finding a satisfactory solution.[8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-8)[9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-9) Heuristics can be mental shortcuts that ease the [cognitive load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load "Cognitive load") of [making a decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-making "Decision-making").[10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-10)[11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-11)[12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-conceptually-12)

> Heuristic reasoning is often based on [induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_reasoning "Inductive reasoning"), or on [analogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogy "Analogy")... Induction is the process of discovering general laws... Induction tries to find regularity and coherence... Its most conspicuous instruments are [generalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalization "Generalization"), [specialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_case "Special case"), analogy.\[...\] Heuristic discusses human behavior in the face of problems \[... that have been\] preserved in the _wisdom of [proverbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proverb "Proverb")_.[13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-13)

— [George Pólya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_P%C3%B3lya "George Pólya"), _[How to Solve It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Solve_It "How to Solve It")_

## Context

Main article: [Strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategy "Strategy")

For broader coverage of this topic, see [Trial and error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial_and_error "Trial and error"), [Rule of thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_thumb "Rule of thumb"), and [Guessing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guessing "Guessing").

Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier (2011) state that [sub-sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_(mathematics) "Set (mathematics)") of _strategy_ include **heuristics**, [regression analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_analysis "Regression analysis"), and [Bayesian inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_inference "Bayesian inference").[14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-14)

> A heuristic is a strategy that ignores part of the information, with the goal of making decisions more quickly, frugally, and/or accurately than more complex methods...[15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-15)

—S. Chow, "Many Meanings of 'Heuristic'", _The British Journal for the Philosophy of Science_

Heuristics are strategies based on rules to generate [optimal decisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_decision "Optimal decision"), like the [anchoring effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchoring_effect "Anchoring effect") and [utility maximization problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_maximization_problem "Utility maximization problem").[16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-16) These strategies depend on using readily accessible, though loosely applicable, information to control [problem solving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_solving "Problem solving") in human beings, machines and abstract issues.[17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-17)[18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-18) When an individual applies a heuristic in practice, it generally performs as expected. However it can alternatively create systematic errors.[19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-19)

The most fundamental heuristic is trial and error, which can be used in everything from matching nuts and bolts to finding the values of variables in algebra problems. In mathematics, some common heuristics involve the use of visual representations, additional assumptions, forward/backward reasoning and simplification.

[Dual process theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_process_theory "Dual process theory") concerns [embodied heuristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embodied_cognition "Embodied cognition").[20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-20)

### Contemporary

The study of heuristics in human [decision-making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-making "Decision-making") was developed in the 1970s and the 1980s, by the psychologists [Amos Tversky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amos_Tversky "Amos Tversky") and [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman "Daniel Kahneman"),[81](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-Kahneman-81) although the concept had been originally introduced by the [Nobel laureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Nobel_laureates "List of Nobel laureates") [Herbert A. Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_A._Simon "Herbert A. Simon"). Simon's original primary object of research was problem solving that showed that we operate within what he calls _[bounded rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_rationality "Bounded rationality")_. He coined the term _[satisficing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisficing "Satisficing")_, which denotes a situation in which people seek solutions, or accept choices or judgements, that are "good enough" for their purposes although they could be optimised.[82](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-82)

[Rudolf Groner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Groner "Rudolf Groner") analysed the history of heuristics from its roots in ancient Greece up to contemporary work in [cognitive psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_psychology "Cognitive psychology") and [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"),[83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-83) proposing a cognitive style "heuristic versus algorithmic thinking", which can be assessed by means of a validated [questionnaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Questionnaire "Questionnaire").[84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-84)

### Adaptive toolbox

The _adaptive toolbox_ contains strategies for fabricating heuristic devices.[85](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-85) The core mental capacities are [recall (memory)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recall_(memory) "Recall (memory)"), [frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency "Frequency"), [object permanence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_permanence "Object permanence"), and [imitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imitation "Imitation").[86](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-86) [Gerd Gigerenzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_Gigerenzer "Gerd Gigerenzer") and his research group argued that models of heuristics need to be formal to allow for predictions of behavior that can be tested.[87](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-87) They study the fast and frugal heuristics in the "adaptive toolbox" of individuals or institutions, and the [ecological rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_rationality "Ecological rationality") of these heuristics; that is, the conditions under which a given heuristic is likely to be successful.[88](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-88) The descriptive study of the "adaptive toolbox" is done by observation and experiment, while the prescriptive study of [ecological rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_rationality "Ecological rationality") requires mathematical analysis and computer simulation. Heuristics – such as the [recognition heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recognition_heuristic "Recognition heuristic"), the [take-the-best heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take-the-best_heuristic "Take-the-best heuristic") and [fast-and-frugal trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast-and-frugal_trees "Fast-and-frugal trees") – have been shown to be effective in predictions, particularly in situations of uncertainty. It is often said that heuristics trade accuracy for effort but this is only the case in situations of risk. Risk refers to situations where all possible actions, their outcomes and probabilities are known. In the absence of this information, that is under uncertainty, heuristics can achieve higher accuracy with lower effort.[89](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-89) This finding, known as a [less-is-more effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Less-is-better_effect "Less-is-better effect"), would not have been found without formal models. The valuable insight of this program is that heuristics are effective not despite their simplicity – but because of it. Furthermore, [Gigerenzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_Gigerenzer "Gerd Gigerenzer") and Wolfgang Gaissmaier found that both individuals and organisations rely on heuristics in an adaptive way.[90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-90)

### Cognitive-experiential self-theory

Heuristics, through greater refinement and research, have begun to be applied to other theories, or be explained by them. For example, the [cognitive-experiential self-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive-experiential_self-theory "Cognitive-experiential self-theory") (CEST) is also an adaptive view of heuristic processing. CEST breaks down two systems that process information. At some times, roughly speaking, individuals consider issues rationally, systematically, logically, deliberately, effortfully, and verbally. On other occasions, individuals consider issues intuitively, effortlessly, globally, and emotionally.[91](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-91) From this perspective, heuristics are part of a larger experiential processing system that is often adaptive, but vulnerable to error in situations that require logical analysis.[92](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-92)

### Attribute substitution

In 2002, [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman "Daniel Kahneman") and [Shane Frederick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Frederick "Shane Frederick") proposed that cognitive heuristics work by a process called _[attribute substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attribute_substitution "Attribute substitution")_, which happens without conscious awareness.[93](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-revisited-93) According to this theory, when somebody makes a judgement (of a "target attribute") that is computationally complex, a more easily calculated "heuristic attribute" is substituted. In effect, a cognitively difficult problem is dealt with by answering a rather simpler problem, without being aware of this happening.[93](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-revisited-93) This theory explains cases where judgements fail to show [regression toward the mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_toward_the_mean "Regression toward the mean").[94](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-94) Heuristics can be considered to reduce the complexity of clinical judgments in health care.[95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-95)

## Academic disciplines

### Psychology

Main article: [Heuristic (psychology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic_(psychology) "Heuristic (psychology)")

For broader coverage of this topic, see [Phenomenology (psychology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_(psychology) "Phenomenology (psychology)").

See also: [Decision-making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-making "Decision-making")

In [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology"), heuristics are simple, efficient rules, either learned or inculcated by evolutionary processes. These psychological heuristics have been proposed to explain how people make decisions, come to judgements, and solve problems. These rules typically come into play when people face complex problems or incomplete information. Researchers employ various methods to test whether people use these rules. The rules have been shown to work well under most circumstances, but in certain cases can lead to systematic errors or [cognitive biases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias "Cognitive bias").[96](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-96)

### Philosophy

A **heuristic device** is used when an entity _X_ exists to enable understanding of, or knowledge concerning, some other entity _Y_.

A good example is a [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_model "Physical model") that, as [it is never identical with what it models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Map/territory_relation "Map/territory relation"), is a heuristic device to enable understanding of what it models. Stories, metaphors, etc., can also be termed heuristic in this sense. A classic example is the notion of [utopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utopia "Utopia") as described in [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato")'s best-known work, _[The Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato's_Republic "Plato's Republic")_. This means that the "ideal city" as depicted in _The Republic_ is not given as something to be pursued, or to present an orientation-point for development. Rather, it shows how things would have to be connected, and how one thing would lead to another (often with highly problematic results), if one opted for certain principles and carried them through rigorously.

_Heuristic_ is also often used as a [noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun "Noun") to describe a [rule of thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_thumb "Rule of thumb"), procedure, or method.[97](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-97) Philosophers of science have emphasised the importance of heuristics in creative thought and the construction of scientific theories.[98](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-98) Seminal works include [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper "Karl Popper")'s _[The Logic of Scientific Discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Logic_of_Scientific_Discovery "The Logic of Scientific Discovery")_ and others by [Imre Lakatos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imre_Lakatos "Imre Lakatos"),[99](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-99) [Lindley Darden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindley_Darden "Lindley Darden"), and [William C. Wimsatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_C._Wimsatt "William C. Wimsatt").

### Law

In [legal theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_theory "Legal theory"), especially in the theory of [law and economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_and_economics "Law and economics"), heuristics are used in the [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law "Law") when [case-by-case analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_method "Case method") would be impractical, insofar as "practicality" is defined by the interests of a governing body.[100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-100)

The present securities regulation regime largely assumes that all investors act as perfectly rational persons. In truth, actual investors face cognitive limitations from biases, heuristics, and framing effects. For instance, in all states in the United States the [legal drinking age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_drinking_age "Legal drinking age") for unsupervised persons is 21 years, because it is argued that people need to be mature enough to make decisions involving the risks of [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_(drug) "Alcohol (drug)") consumption. However, assuming people mature at different rates, the specific age of 21 would be too late for some and too early for others. In this case, the somewhat arbitrary delineation is used because it is impossible or impractical to tell whether an individual is sufficiently mature for society to trust them with that kind of responsibility. Some proposed changes, however, have included the completion of an alcohol education course rather than the attainment of 21 years of age as the criterion for legal alcohol possession. This would put youth alcohol policy more on a case-by-case basis and less on a heuristic one, since the completion of such a course would presumably be voluntary and not uniform across the population.

The same reasoning applies to [patent law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent_law "Patent law"). [Patents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent "Patent") are justified on the grounds that inventors must be protected so they have incentive to invent. It is therefore argued that it is in society's best interest that inventors receive a temporary government-granted [monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly "Monopoly") on their idea, so that they can recoup investment costs and make economic profit for a limited period. In the United States, the length of this temporary monopoly is 20 years from the date the patent application was filed, though the monopoly does not actually begin until the application has matured into a patent. However, like the drinking age problem above, the specific length of time would need to be different for every product to be efficient. A 20-year term is used because it is difficult to tell what the number should be for any individual patent. More recently, some, including [University of North Dakota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_North_Dakota "University of North Dakota") law professor Eric E. Johnson, have argued that patents in different kinds of industries – such as [software patents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_patent "Software patent") – should be protected for different lengths of time.[101](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-101)

### Artificial intelligence

The [bias–variance tradeoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bias%E2%80%93variance_tradeoff "Bias–variance tradeoff") gives insight into describing the less-is-more strategy.[102](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-102) A [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic_(computer_science) "Heuristic (computer science)") can be used in [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") systems while searching a [solution space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_space "Solution space"). The heuristic is derived by using some function that is put into the system by the designer, or by adjusting the weight of branches based on how likely each branch is to lead to a [goal node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goal_node_(computer_science) "Goal node (computer science)").

### Behavioural economics

Heuristics refers to the cognitive shortcuts that individuals use to simplify decision-making processes in economic situations. [Behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics "Behavioral economics") is a field that integrates insights from psychology and economics to better understand how people make decisions.

Anchoring and adjustment is one of the most extensively researched heuristics in behavioural economics. Anchoring is the tendency of people to make future judgements or conclusions based too heavily on the original information supplied to them. This initial knowledge functions as an anchor, and it can influence future judgements even if the anchor is entirely unrelated to the decisions at hand. Adjustment, on the other hand, is the process through which individuals make gradual changes to their initial judgements or conclusions.

[Anchoring and adjustment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchoring_and_adjustment "Anchoring and adjustment") has been observed in a wide range of decision-making contexts, including financial decision-making, consumer behavior, and negotiation. Researchers have identified a number of strategies that can be used to mitigate the effects of anchoring and adjustment, including providing multiple anchors, encouraging individuals to generate alternative anchors, and providing cognitive prompts to encourage more deliberative decision-making.

Other heuristics studied in behavioral economics include the [representativeness heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representativeness_heuristic "Representativeness heuristic"), which refers to the tendency of individuals to categorize objects or events based on how similar they are to typical examples,[103](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-103) and the [availability heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Availability_heuristic "Availability heuristic"), which refers to the tendency of individuals to judge the likelihood of an event based on how easily it comes to mind.[104](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-104)

## Stereotyping

[Stereotyping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype "Stereotype") is a type of heuristic that people use to form opinions or make judgements about things they have never seen or experienced.[105](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-105) They work as a mental shortcut to assess everything from the social status of a person (based on their actions),[12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-conceptually-12) to classifying a plant as a tree based on it being tall, having a trunk, and that it has leaves (even though the person making the evaluation might never have seen that particular type of tree before).

Stereotypes, as first described by journalist [Walter Lippmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Lippmann "Walter Lippmann") in his book _[Public Opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Opinion_(book) "Public Opinion (book)")_ (1922), are the pictures we have in our heads that are built around experiences as well as what we are told about the world.[106](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-106)[107](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic#cite_note-107)
